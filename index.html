A bag of sand that must never see daylight.

It comes out of a rock shelter called Madjedbebe, on Mirarr Country in Arnhem Land. Inside the sand is a clock. Every grain has been counting since the last time the sun touched it.

Open the bag in the light, and the clock is wiped.

In 2017 the people who dug it wrote down what the clock said.

Nature · 20 July 2017

“The time of arrival of people in Australia is an unresolved question. … Here we report the results of new excavations conducted at Madjedbebe, a rock shelter in northern Australia. … Human occupation began around 65,000 years ago, with a distinctive stone tool assemblage including grinding stones, ground ochres, reflective additives and ground-edge hatchet heads. This evidence sets a new minimum age for the arrival of humans in Australia …”

Source. Chris Clarkson, Zenobia Jacobs, Ben Marwick and others, “Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago”, Nature 547 (2017), 306–310, doi:10.1038/nature22968.

Sixty-five thousand years. A year later, other scientists said: not so fast.

Then, far to the west, a dinner plate. Hammered flat. Nailed to a post on a cliff at Shark Bay. Letters scratched in with a knife.

Two days on the island. Then gone.

Thirteen years later, a ship full of silver hits a reef in the dark. Hundreds of people are stranded on bare islands with almost no water. The man in charge sails away to get help.

By the time he comes back, about 125 people are dead.

He wasn’t there. But his journal is most of what we have.

Three cases. Nine witnesses. Some were there. Some weren’t. Some can’t talk.

What is agreed

1 · Deep Time
Draft, pending consultation
  • People lived on this continent tens of thousands of years before any European ship arrived.
  • At Madjedbebe, on Mirarr Country, excavations in 2012 and 2015 found stone tools, grinding stones and ground ochre. The 2017 paper dates the first occupation to around 65,000 years ago and calls this “a new minimum age”. A 2018 review argues the site is younger, and puts arrival in the region at about 50,000–55,000 years ago.
  • The dig was carried out with the permission of the Mirarr Senior Traditional Owners and the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation.
  • At Lake Mungo, in south-western New South Wales, Jim Bowler found the cremated remains of a woman in 1968 and the burial of a man in 1974. Both are dated to between 40,000 and 42,000 years ago, the oldest human remains found in Australia. Mungo Lady was returned in 1992. Mungo Man was returned to Country, to the Paakantji, Ngiyampaa and Mutthi Mutthi peoples, on 17 November 2017.
2 · Cape Inscription, 1616
Draft, pending consultation
  • The Dutch East India Company ship Eendracht reached Shark Bay, on Malgana Country, on 25 October 1616, and sailed for Bantam on 27 October.
  • The crew left an inscribed pewter plate on a post at what is now Cape Inscription, on Dirk Hartog Island.
  • In February 1697 Willem de Vlamingh found the plate, took it to Amsterdam, and left a new plate with the 1616 words copied and his own added. The 1616 plate is in the Rijksmuseum; the 1697 plate is in the Western Australian Museum.

3 · Batavia, 1629
Draft, pending consultation
  • The VOC ship Batavia left Texel in late October 1628 (the 1647 printed journal says 28 October) bound for Batavia, now Jakarta.
  • It struck Morning Reef in the Houtman Abrolhos in the early hours of 4 June 1629. Between about 316 and 322 people were aboard. About forty drowned; most of the rest reached the islands.
  • Pelsaert, the skipper Ariaen Jacobsz and a crew left in the longboat to look for water, then sailed to Batavia, arriving on 7 July. On 14 June, on the mainland coast, Pelsaert recorded smoke inland and four people who ran from his men.
  • While he was away, Jeronimus Cornelisz led a mutiny. About 125 men, women and children were killed (Western Australian Museum). Soldiers under Wiebbe Hayes held out on a larger island and warned Pelsaert when the Sardam arrived in mid-September.
  • On 2 October 1629 Cornelisz and seven others were hanged on Seals Island (Long Island). Wouter Loos and Jan Pelgrom de Bye were put ashore on the mainland, near the mouth of the Murchison River according to the Western Australian Museum. Nothing more was heard of them.
  • Pelsaert’s journal survives in the Nationaal Archief. The ship’s pay books are lost. The wreck was found in 1963.

Pelsaert’s journal · 14 June 1629

“In the afternoon seeing some smoke inland, rowed thither hoping to find an opportunity of landing. Were quite rejoiced for I imagined that where there were people there would also be fresh water. … yet 6 men swam ashore … Our folk was searching for water and for nothing else until nightfall but found none, saw also four men creeping towards them on hands and feet, when our folk, coming out of a hollow upon a height, approached them suddenly, they leapt to their feet and fled full speed, which was clearly seen by us in the boat.”

The Madjedbebe excavation team

Archaeologists and dating scientists · Nature, 20 July 2017

Source. Chris Clarkson, Zenobia Jacobs, Ben Marwick and others, “Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago”, Nature 547 (2017), 306–310, doi:10.1038/nature22968.

See the source (Nature) ↗

What did they say they saw? “The time of arrival of people in Australia is an unresolved question. … Here we report the results of new excavations conducted at Madjedbebe, a rock shelter in northern Australia. … Human occupation began around 65,000 years ago, with a distinctive stone tool assemblage including grinding stones, ground ochres, reflective additives and ground-edge hatchet heads. This evidence sets a new minimum age for the arrival of humans in Australia …”

Could they really have seen it? They dug the rock shelter in 2012 and 2015. They checked how broken stone pieces fitted back together, and they dated the sand in more than one way. They saw the layers themselves. They could not see the people who made the tools.

Why might they say it? To report what they found. A find like this is also important to the people who make it. Someone can have a reason to say something and still be right.

The 2018 review team

Archaeologists and geologists · PNAS, August 2018

Source. James F. O’Connell, Jim Allen, Martin A. J. Williams and others, “When did Homo sapiens first reach Southeast Asia and Sahul?”, PNAS 115, no. 34 (2018), 8482–8490, doi:10.1073/pnas.1808385115.

See the source (PNAS) ↗

What did they say they saw? “We find that an age estimate of >50 ka for this site is unlikely to be valid.” They argued the ground could have been disturbed, so tools might have moved down into older sand, and they put people’s arrival in this region at about 50,000–55,000 years ago.

Could they really have seen it? They did not dig the site. They reviewed the published reports and data.

Why might they say it? Checking other people’s dates is part of how science works. They also had their own view of when people spread out of Africa.

The Mirarr Traditional Owners

Custodians of Madjedbebe · recorded in the 2017 paper

Source. Chris Clarkson, Zenobia Jacobs, Ben Marwick and others, “Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago”, Nature 547 (2017), 306–310, doi:10.1038/nature22968. Acknowledgements. The names of the two Senior Traditional Owners are omitted here.

What did they say they saw? This app does not put words in their mouths. The written record here is the paper’s own acknowledgement: “The authors are grateful to the custodians of Madjedbebe, the Mirarr Senior Traditional Owners … and our research partners (Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation) for permission to carry out this research and publish this paper.”

Could they really have seen it? It is their Country. Their knowledge of it is theirs to share, in their own words and their own way.

Why might they say it? The paper records that permission was given. It does not record their reasons. What is missing from this card is part of the evidence.

Willem de Vlamingh

Dutch captain · visited Cape Inscription, February 1697

Source. Vlamingh plate, 1697, Western Australian Museum, as drawn in The Cyclopedia of Western Australia, ed. J. S. Battye (Perth, 1912). National Library of Australia, nla.obj-250466531. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

What did they say they saw? He found the 1616 plate still on its post, took it back to Amsterdam, and left a new plate with the old words copied onto it. His own words begin: 1697 DEN 4 FEBREVARY IS HIER AEN GECOMEN HET SCHIP DE GEELVINCK VOOR AMSTERDAM DEN COMANDEUR ENDE SCHIPPER WILLEM DE VLAMINGH VAN VLIELANDT … (In English: “1697, on 4 February, arrived here the ship Geelvinck of Amsterdam; the commander and skipper Willem de Vlamingh of Vlieland …”)

Could they really have seen it? He saw the 1616 plate 81 years after it was made. He did not see the 1616 landing.

Why might they say it? To keep the old record, to send the original home, and to add a record of his own voyage.

Francisco Pelsaert

Commandeur of the Batavia · wrote the journal · died 1630

Source. Francisco Pelsaert, Batavia journal, 1629, Nationaal Archief, The Hague, VOC archive (1.04.02), inv. nr. 1098 (QQ II), fols 232–316. English translation: Marit van Huystee, The Batavia Journal of Francisco Pelsaert, Western Australian Maritime Museum Report No. 136 (1994).

See the source (van Huystee translation, WA Museum) ↗

What did they say they saw? His journal records the wreck, the longboat search for water, the rescue and the trials. On 14 June 1629 he wrote: “In the afternoon seeing some smoke inland, rowed thither hoping to find an opportunity of landing. Were quite rejoiced for I imagined that where there were people there would also be fresh water. … yet 6 men swam ashore … Our folk was searching for water and for nothing else until nightfall but found none, saw also four men creeping towards them on hands and feet, when our folk, coming out of a hollow upon a height, approached them suddenly, they leapt to their feet and fled full speed, which was clearly seen by us in the boat.”

Could they really have seen it? He was there for the wreck and the rescue. For the weeks of the killings he was away, in the longboat and in Batavia. What he wrote about those weeks came from questions he asked afterwards, and from confessions, some given under torture, which the company’s law allowed.

Why might they say it? He had lost the company’s ship and much of its cargo, and he was writing for the company.

Wiebbe Hayes

Soldier · led the group that held the higher island

Source. Known mainly through Pelsaert’s journal (Francisco Pelsaert, Batavia journal, 1629, Nationaal Archief, The Hague, VOC archive (1.04.02), inv. nr. 1098 (QQ II), fols 232–316. English translation: Marit van Huystee, The Batavia Journal of Francisco Pelsaert, Western Australian Maritime Museum Report No. 136 (1994).) No journal of Hayes’s own is known to survive.

See the source (van Huystee translation, WA Museum) ↗

What did they say they saw? We do not have his own words. Pelsaert recorded that Hayes’s group found water, held out against the mutineers, and that Hayes reached the Sardam first.

Could they really have seen it? He was on the islands for all the weeks Pelsaert missed, and saw his own island. He could not see everything on the other islands.

Why might they say it? He needed the rescuers to hear his group’s side first. Afterwards he was rewarded. His story reaches us through someone else’s pen.

The wreck of the Batavia

The ship itself · found 1963 · timbers now in the Shipwreck Galleries, Fremantle

Source. Western Australian Museum, Shipwreck Galleries, Fremantle: stern timbers and stone portico. Engraving: Ongeluckige voyagie, van ’t schip Batavia (Amsterdam: Jan Jansz, 1647). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (scanned from the Hordern House facsimile, 1994).

Image (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

What did they say they saw? The objects do not speak. The timbers, the stone gateway that was cargo, coins, jugs and cannon show that the ship was there and what it carried.

Could they really have seen it? They were on the reef. They never saw the islands, so they cannot tell us what was said or done there.

Why might they say it? Objects have no reasons. They confirm the wreck. They do not fill the gaps in the written record.

Why it still matters

Arguments about land in Australia have often turned on what was written down by newcomers, and what was not. A plate that records a ship but not the people on the shore, or a journal written by a man who was not there, can still shape what later people believe happened. Deep-time dates are still being argued over in science journals today. Mungo Man was only returned to Country in 2017. These records are not finished.

Conflicts and source issues to flag.

  • Madjedbebe: about 65,000 years (Clarkson et al., Nature, 2017) against the 2018 review (O’Connell et al., PNAS), which argues an age over 50,000 years “is unlikely to be valid” and puts arrival at about 50,000–55,000 years. Later papers continue the debate on both sides.
  • Lake Mungo: dates are 40,000–42,000 years (National Museum of Australia). One news report (BBC, 17 November 2017) says Mungo Lady was found in 1967; the National Museum and the Australian Dictionary of Biography give 1968.
  • Hartog plate: spellings differ between the original (WA Museum transcription: “D’EENDRAGHT”, “MIBAIS”, “LVICK”, “DOOKES”) and the 1697 copy as drawn in 1912 (“EENDRACHT”, “MIEBAIS”, “LUCK”, “DOORES”). “Bil” is usually read as Brielle.
  • Batavia: people on board are given as 316 (WA Museum) or about 322 (other sources). Totals for the number killed differ between accounts; this app uses the Western Australian Museum’s count of about 125.
  • Pelsaert’s journal reference is Nationaal Archief VOC 1.04.02, inv. 1098 (QQ II), fols 232–316 (as cited by the WA Museum and State Library of WA). An earlier draft gave fol. 317.
  • The 1647 book is not Pelsaert’s handwriting; it was printed after his death, and the engravings were made by people who were not there.
  • Much of the mutiny record comes from confessions, some taken under torture, written down by Pelsaert, who was not on the islands at the time.
  • Where Loos and Pelgrom landed is not certain. The WA Museum says near the mouth of the Murchison River; other places have been proposed.
  • Pelsaert’s 14 June entry goes on to describe the four people with period language. The student extract stops before that sentence; the full text is in the van Huystee translation.
  • The Mirarr Senior Traditional Owners are named in the 2017 paper; their names are left out here as a matter of care.
  • The opener uses scene-setting language but only real documents. No reconstructions are used.
